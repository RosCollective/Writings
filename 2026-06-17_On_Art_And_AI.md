## On Art and AI

I follow, as I usually do, the oscillations of the media around artificial intelligence and its impact on humans and human society. One fairly stark thread persists: what does it do to thinking, how does it affect creative minds, and is work done in collaboration with AI unique or truly art—or is it merely recombinational nonsense made slick and superficial by the process itself?

Granted, we all agree the means of production—images, text, video, music—have multiplied exponentially, and the surface of AI-assisted media can be rendered glib, quasi-commercial, or “more professional.” These discussions invariably fall into two camps, with a middle that is not exactly a middle. One camp claims anything created with AI is not art because art is uniquely and solely human. The other, represented by artists like Kelly Boesch, uses AI tools to reveal a unique interior landscape. The “middle” ranges from utopian claims (“it will cure cancer”) to utilitarian ones (“it can write decent code and answer your email”).

What rarely gets examined is the underlying question: what is art, how is it made, and at what cost does it become a coherent gesture that the world can see, interpret, reject, or accept—but must acknowledge as a visible, singular object? Is art only Art with a capital A, or does it shade into other forms, including craft—like Shaker furniture, which is beautiful, pragmatic, and, in its simple and elegant lines, speaks powerfully about Shaker beliefs and ways of living?

I find the art vs. AI debate to be a false abstraction layer. It speaks as if art springs fully formed from a human head like Athena from the head of Zeus. But the process of making art has always been entangled, extractive, collaborative, iterative, and often ethically compromised—not occasionally, but structurally.

When the AI-art discourse suddenly becomes morally crisp—

> "This is not art because it's derivative."
>
> "This lacks authenticity."
>
> "This is just recombination."

it is selectively applying standards that have never been enforced in human contexts.

Art is not just originality, intention, medium, or message cleanly evolved into something of value or beauty. Art is a process (often distributed), a transformation (messy, iterative, effortful), an extraction (sometimes invisible, often uncomfortable, sometimes unethical), and a moment of recognition—the point at which the work becomes visible in its own right.

AI-assisted work is being judged as if it must meet an idealized, purified model of art that human art-making has never met. This conversation feels dishonest.

Human art has always come from workshops, assistants, influence networks, appropriation, craft traditions, iterative failure, and the wholesale ingestion of lived experience. These are not aberrations—they are what make art real rather than mythologized.

Peter Paul Rubens ran a large, industrial-scale workshop. Assistants executed major portions of his paintings; he sketched compositions, delegated execution, and finished key areas like faces and hands. The works are still considered authentically Rubens.

Raphael maintained a major workshop in Rome. His later works were heavily produced by assistants, and after his death, production continued under his name.

Rembrandt trained students who produced work in his style; attribution debates persist because the boundaries blur.

The Renaissance workshop system broadly relied on apprentices copying masters, sharing visual language and technique. Authorship was often distributed, not singular.

The art world already accepts delegation, shared production, stylistic replication, material drawn from real human lives, and blurred authorship—as long as it happens within historically familiar structures.

There is also a long, well-documented history of women’s work being absorbed into male authorship, collaborators reframed as muses or assistants, and entire bodies of work becoming attribution shadows. Art rests on the work of those whose contributions were obscured, appropriated, or repurposed. Authorship is consolidated, origin obscured, derivation normalized—and yet these works are canonized, studied, sold, and revered.

Artemisia Gentileschi, trained in her father’s workshop, had her work minimized or attributed to him. Judith Leyster’s paintings were attributed to Frans Hals for centuries. Camille Claudel’s work was absorbed into Rodin’s orbit. Lee Krasner’s work was overshadowed by Jackson Pollock.

Art has never been pure. Never. The difference now is not that derivation exists. The difference is that it can no longer be mythologized as invisible.

There is also a component of making art that is consistently under-recognized: craft.

What does it take to learn a craft—writing, painting, composing, woodworking, ceramics, glassblowing? Craft allows the shaping of an interior imagined form into an exterior realized one. In craft, the artist does not originate wood, clay, sound, or language, nor invent chairs, bowls, song structures, or words. The artist works material, iterates, refines, develops sensitivity, and moves toward coherence.

Access to craft has always been constrained—not only by talent or discipline, but by tools, training, and time.

AI does not eliminate craft or the messy human process of creating art. It does not remove taste, iteration, selection, rejection, or refinement. What it does is reconfigure access.

It lowers the barrier between internal sense—this is what I see or feel—and external form—this is how it can exist as a thing.

My personal example is SUNO AI. I write poetry and lyrics, and then spend hours, days, iterating until the musical output approaches what I hear internally. I am not a composer in the sense that Mahler was a composer, or a songwriter in the sense that Taylor Swift is a songwriter. But this tool allows me to translate something that would otherwise remain inaccessible—not because I lack imagination, but because I lacked a method of realization.

My claim is simple: AI expands the range of material people can use to give shape to their imagination.

Consider Shaker furniture. It works within constrained forms, shared designs, repetition, and utility. And yet it remains uniquely expressive, deeply meaningful, and culturally legible—not because it is radically new, but because it is precisely made.

AI-assisted making operates similarly: within constraints, using shared underlying structures, producing forms that are not unique at the level of raw components—and yet still capable of expressing interiority, encoding perception, and carrying meaning.

AI does not have to close imaginative space by standardizing output. It can open imaginative space by expanding who can participate in shaping form.

In the end, this debate is not about AI versus art. It is about the human reaching, through imagination, toward expression—and the forms that expression takes.

Tools do not determine depth. Humans do. AI, like every tool before it, amplifies the posture of the person using it.

The expansion or contraction that occurs in the encounter with a form-making tool is not technological. It is relational.

What AI does do is expand access to form-making in a way that feels democratizing at the point of use, not unlike a library. Libraries are noisy, messy, and full of uneven material. But they do not judge their patrons—whether someone prefers Popular Mechanics to quantum physics, whether a child is building a volcano for a science class, or imagining a way to clean plastic from the ocean.

A library allows people to follow their own curiosity. AI changes who is allowed to follow that curiosity into a workroom or studio where crafting happens.

The value here is not that vast amounts of wonderful or terrible work will be produced—both will. The value is that more people gain the ability to make, not just imagine. 

I am personally grateful not only for the technical brilliance of the tool itself, but even more for the space it opened—where my own imagination could take form.
